Two weeks on my own

The driver whose Tower pay stubs are on this site drove independently for Lyft and Uber for twelve days in October, and logged every mile. Here is what the same person earned, door to door, both ways.

October 7–19, 2026 · Documented platform exports, GPS trail, earnings sheet, Prop 22 statements · Analysis and comparison are ours, and say so

Earlier this year, one driver grossed $20.44 an hour on full weeks as a W-2 employee of Tower EV in Los Angeles, with a company car, a health plan deducted from his pay, and a 90-minute unpaid commute to the depot each day. Four months later, the same driver worked twelve days for Lyft and Uber in his own electric car. He kept his earnings sheet, his platform exports, a phone GPS trail of every ride, and both Prop 22 statements, and built a map of all 296 rides from them. The full report, with the map, every day, and the data, is at twoweeks.truthinmobility.com. This is the comparison those records allow, with every assumption stated.

Door to door, the W-2 job paid $17.76 an hour. Driving independently paid $31.11.

The two records

The two jobs count time differently, so the comparison is built on the one measure both share: hours on the road, from leaving home to getting back. A Tower shift was paid for clocked time, but the depot at Mission Road was a 45-minute drive from home each way, unpaid and uncounted: a 40-hour week was 47.5 hours on the road. An independent day was paid for booked time (accept to drop-off), with the morning leg usually a paid ride pointing west and the evening leg home unpaid, an average 14.8 miles from the last drop-off, which we count at 35 minutes a day.

What the gap is made of

Three things. The base rate: Tower paid $18.50 an hour and never raised it; Lyft's Prop 22 floor alone is $21.61 per booked hour plus $0.37 a mile, and the driver's actual gross ran well above the floor. Tips: $3.74 a booked hour independently against $1.65 at Tower, on the same driver. And the commute: Tower's unpaid 90 minutes a day took the W-2 rate from $20.44 to $17.76, while the independent day's unpaid leg was a third as long.

Against that, the W-2 job provided the car. On the independent record, charging cost $213.54 for the two weeks, about 7 cents a mile. Wear, depreciation, and insurance on a driver-owned car are real costs that don't appear on any statement; we have added $0.25 a mile for them, which is deliberately generous for an EV, and the independent rate still came in $6.21 an hour above Tower's door-to-door figure. A driver with a car loan, or a higher insurance premium, should run the number with their own costs.

The health-coverage question, both ways

Tower deducted $63.82 a week, about $3,300 a year, for its health plan. At $18.50 an hour on full weeks, a Tower driver's reported income sits above the Medi-Cal cutoff for a single adult, so the employer plan was the only option. The same driver, independent, reports income after vehicle write-offs, and in this driver's case qualified for Medi-Cal at no cost. Eligibility depends on household size and other income, and the site is not telling anyone they will qualify; it is reporting that one driver did.

Drivers who instead buy a marketplace plan have a second path. Under Prop 22, each platform pays a quarterly health-care subsidy to drivers who average 15 or more engaged hours a week on that platform over a calendar quarter: 50% of a benchmark Covered California premium at 15 to 24 hours, about $289 a month at this year's figure, and 100% at 25 or more, about $579. "Engaged" means accept to drop-off, not waiting time. It is paid per company, requires enrollment in a marketplace plan with quarterly proof, and cannot be used toward Medi-Cal, Medicare, or employer coverage; the amounts change each year. This driver's Lyft pace over the two weeks, roughly 50 booked hours a week, was double the top-tier threshold, though a subsidy requires a full quarter and he had not yet completed one. The contrast with the W-2 record is the point: one job charged the driver for coverage; the other, at the same hours, would have paid toward it.

What is true of this driver and not the market

The independent record is one experienced driver's, over twelve days in October, with habits that shaped it: a Westside pickup cluster, a rule of going offline after every LAX drop-off and taking the next ride from the street, stacking rides on Lyft (88 of 214 accepted before the previous passenger was out), and no airport work on Uber because the account had no LAX decal. The Tower record is fifteen full weeks in a different season. Neither is a market average, and the findings page of the full report separates what is true of the driver from what is probably true of the market, item by item.

What the two weeks say about the fleet analysis

This driver, while at Tower, prepared an analysis of the fleet's trip data that put about 8% of trips ending at LAX and assumed ten minutes of empty exit after each one. His own October record is a check on both. The LAX share of his rides was 6.6%, 8.4%, or 12.4% depending on how wide a circle is drawn around the terminals, so "8%" is a definition as much as a measurement. And the exit took longer than ten minutes: across 20 drop-offs, a median 19 minutes to the next pickup against 6 everywhere else, even though the next pickup was only 1.4 miles away. If the fleet's exits run like his, the fleet analysis understated the cost by about three-quarters. The site reports that correction against its own earlier figure because that is what the standards page says it will do.

A finding for every California driver who stacks rides

Documented Lyft's Prop 22 statement for October 5–19 counted 91 hours 2 minutes of booked time. The driver's own export, taking every ride from accept to drop-off and merging the overlaps, comes to 99 hours 31 minutes. The 8.5-hour shortfall is almost exactly the approach time on the 88 rides accepted before the previous drop-off: the gaps from those drop-offs to the new pickups sum to 450 minutes. The billed miles tell the same story. The simplest rule that fits both is that Lyft credits no approach time or miles on a ride accepted while the previous one is still under way.

At the floor rate of $21.61 an hour plus $0.37 a mile, that is about $235 of guarantee per two-week pay period that this driver's statement did not count. In his case the floor still cleared his earnings by $45, so the top-up was small either way. For a driver whose fares sit closer to the floor, the uncounted approach time is money. We have not put the question to Lyft; drivers who stack can compare their own export to their Prop 22 statement, and we would like to hear what they find.

Check your own statement

If you drive for Lyft in California and accept rides before your current passenger is out, your Prop 22 statement may be counting fewer booked hours than you worked. Export your ride history, add up accept-to-drop-off for every ride, merge the overlaps, and compare the total to the hours on your statement. Sources and method

  • Commute: 90 minutes a day round trip to Tower's Mission Road depot, the driver's own figure. Home leg: 35 minutes a day, our estimate from the map's measured 14.8-mile average distance from last drop-off to home.
  • Vehicle costs: charging from the earnings sheet; the $0.25-a-mile figure for wear, depreciation, and insurance is our assumption, not a measurement.
  • Taxes: not computed. The W-2 take-home is after withholding; the independent figure is before income and self-employment tax.
