Truth in Mobility

The car can drive itself. The rent can't pay itself.

A worker-aligned publication on the rideshare economy: who is automating it, who profits, and what happens to the people who do the driving.

Rideshare is being rebuilt without drivers. Not in some distant future, and not by robots alone. It is being rebuilt by companies with names you know and companies you have never heard of: the platforms, the robotaxi operators, and the fleet operators in between who already own the depots, the chargers, and the cars. We report on all of them, and we take the drivers' side.

We are not fighting the technology. The sensors and software that make a Waymo safer should keep getting better. We are fighting a business plan that takes that technology and points it at a single cost line: the human being behind the wheel.

The scale of it

5,000,000

That is how many American driving jobs the industry's own forecasters say self-driving vehicles could eliminate. Not the Teamsters' number. RethinkX, a firm that champions the disruption, puts it at up to 5 million, about 3% of all U.S. employment.

Those jobs support households. At two to three people each, that is on the order of 10 to 15 million Americans, more than every resident of Los Angeles County, more than the five boroughs of New York, facing a recession of their own while the companies that caused it report record bookings. That is a multiplier, not a count; RethinkX's figure is a scenario, published in an earlier report, and the firm also projects new jobs elsewhere. Government economists concluded that workers in dedicated driving occupations may have difficulty finding alternative employment, because they are, on average, older and have fewer transferable skills. One analysis put the wealth loss to affected workers and their communities at $100 to $200 billion, and up to $300 billion if the losses land during a recession.

The industry's defenders will tell you this unfolds over a decade, as if that softens it. A decade is a fifth of a working life. It is the window in which a driver loses the house, the kids finish school on a shrinking budget, and the retirement account gets emptied to cover rent. A slow collapse is still a collapse. It just comes with no headline.

It has already started. In cities where robotaxis operate, hourly pay for Uber and Lyft drivers fell between mid-2024 and mid-2025, steepest in San Francisco (down 6.9%) and Austin, while pay in cities without robotaxis rose slightly. Waymo took an estimated 15% of rideshare gross bookings in San Francisco and in Los Angeles in June 2026, per YipitData estimates reported by Business Insider; those are booking dollars within Waymo's service areas, not rides, and they are estimates. The pay declines are associations, not a measured cause. No funded transition plan for the drivers on the other side of that number has been enacted anywhere robotaxis operate.

The safety data tells you how a robotaxi performs on the road. It tells you nothing about what happens to the family whose rent it used to pay.

Andres Munoz Doesn't Want You Driving His Cars

Tower Mobility tells drivers it pays $25 an hour plus benefits. It told an autonomous-vehicle newsletter it is building fleet management for an AV future. Current and former Los Angeles employees describe 150,000-mile Teslas, four Uber Black-eligible cars, a trash pile, and drivers told that declining a depot transfer would be treated as a resignation. Their observations date from February to October 2026. Before Tower manages tomorrow's robotaxis, look at how it manages the fleet it already has.
