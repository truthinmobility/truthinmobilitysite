California

The fight is winnable here

The legislature has already voted, twice, by lopsided margins, to put a human in the vehicle. One man vetoed it both times. He leaves office in January.

California is where robotaxis were born and where the rules get written for everyone else. It is also where organized drivers have come closest to winning. Here is the record, with sources, and what we are asking for next.

The pattern

Documented In 2023, AB 316, which would have required a trained human operator in any autonomous vehicle over 10,000 pounds, passed the Assembly 69 to 4 and the Senate 36 to 2. Governor Newsom vetoed it, calling it unnecessary. The legislature passed a near-identical bill, AB 2286, the following year. He vetoed that too. California has not overridden a governor's veto since 1980, so the bills died on his desk.

Then, in April 2026, his DMV did what the legislature had refused to allow: it adopted rules opening California's roads to heavy driverless trucks. The California Teamsters sued the state in August, arguing the agency used an abbreviated process and hid the economic cost. That suit is pending.

The robotaxi side has been harder. SB 915, which would have let cities and counties issue their own permits, cap the number of robotaxis, and set hours and pickup rules, died in committee in 2024 after industry lobbyists argued it amounted to a ban. Today, cities have no authority to cap the number of robotaxis on their streets or to permit the services; the DMV and CPUC do. The DMV's 2026 rules give first responders emergency geofencing powers, which is a different thing.

The next governor decides

Documented Driverless vehicles have become a litmus test in the governor's race, with several Democrats signaling they would slow or rein in autonomous trucking. Xavier Becerra has said in public that driverless trucks "must take a backseat to jobs and safety." We have not found a public position from Steve Hilton on robotaxis or autonomous trucks, and we have not found one from Becerra on robotaxis specifically, as opposed to trucks.

That gap is our first campaign. We intend to put the same five questions to both candidates in writing before November 3, and to publish their answers or their silence.

Five questions for the next governor

  1. Will you sign a bill requiring a trained human operator in autonomous vehicles over 10,000 pounds?
  2. Will you withdraw or rewrite the DMV's 2026 heavy-truck rules pending the outcome of the Teamsters' lawsuit?
  3. Will you support giving cities and counties the authority to cap the number of robotaxis on their streets and set hours and pickup rules?
  4. Will you require robotaxi operators to publish utilization data: how many of their cars carry a paying passenger, by hour and city?
  5. What is your plan for the drivers displaced by autonomous vehicles in California: funded, with a timeline, not a stakeholder process?

What we are asking for

  1. A human operator where the vehicle is heavy or the passenger is vulnerable. Trucks over 10,000 pounds. Wheelchair-accessible and medical transport. Airport service. The legislature has already voted for the first of these twice.
  2. Local control. Cities and counties can regulate taxis. They should be able to regulate robotaxis: caps, hours, curb rules, and the power to pause a fleet that is blocking emergency vehicles.
  3. Utilization transparency. Any company permitted to run driverless passenger service publishes occupancy and utilization data quarterly. If the cars are full, the data will show it.
  4. A driver's-side earnings record. Platforms report driver net earnings, by city and quarter, to the state. Robotaxi expansion decisions are made with that data on the table.
  5. Transition money with teeth. A displacement fund paid for by the companies doing the displacing, not a study, not a commission, not a stakeholder process.
  6. Driver-assist, not driver-replace. State incentives for fleets that equip human-driven vehicles with the same sensor suites and safety software, and publish the safety results.

Who is already in this fight

We are a publication, not a union. The organizing is being done by people who have been at it longer than we have. If you drive in California, start here:

  • Rideshare Drivers United, which counts roughly 20,000 California drivers as members and has allied with Teamsters 856 on AV guardrails.
  • Teamsters Joint Council 7 and Joint Council 42, who carried AB 316 and AB 2286 through the legislature and filed the DMV lawsuit.
  • Your city council and county supervisors. Los Angeles, Oakland, and the counties of San Francisco, Los Angeles, and San Mateo all passed resolutions supporting local control in 2024. Ask yours whether they still do.
